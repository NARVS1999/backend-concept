### Format: Close Reading + Thematic Analysis (bawat chapter may study task)

## How to Use This Guide

Ang goal dito ay hindi lang maalala kung ano ang nangyari sa bawat chapter — kundi maintindihan ang deeper meaning ng characters, symbols, at themes na ginamit ni Harper Lee. Bago mag-move sa susunod na chapter:

1. Nabasa mo na ang chapter (kung hindi pa, basahin muna — huwag umasa lang sa summary)
2. Nasagot mo ang study task sa sarili mong salita (hindi copy-paste)
3. May notes ka tungkol sa isang key detail na tumatak sa iyo

## PART ONE: Childhood in Maycomb (Chapters 1–8)
*Introduction to Maycomb, the Finch family, and the mystery of Boo Radley. Establishing the world bago pumasok ang larger conflict.*

### Chapter 1 — The Radley Place

**Focus**
First-person narrator Scout Finch introduces Maycomb, Alabama during the Depression. She lives with her father Atticus (a lawyer) and older brother Jem. They meet Dill, a summer visitor obsessed with the Radley Place — a haunted house occupied by the mysterious Boo Radley, a recluse who hasn't been seen in years.

**Key Details**
- Narrator: Jean Louise "Scout" Finch (age ~6), told retrospectively by adult Scout
- Setting: Maycomb, Alabama, 1930s — tired, old, poor Southern town
- Atticus: widower, respected lawyer, unusually old parent (~50)
- Jem: 10 years old, protective older brother
- Calpurnia: Finch cook and housekeeper, strict but loving
- Dill (Charles Baker Harris): based on Harper Lee's childhood friend Truman Capote
- Boo Radley (Arthur Radley): local legend — stabbed his father with scissors, rarely leaves home
- Simon Finch: ancestor who founded Finch's Landing
- Narrative technique: Gothic elements — the closed shutters, the folklore, the mystery
- "Boo was about six-and-a-half feet tall... he dined on raw squirrels"

**Study Task:** What specific details make the Radley Place frightening to the children? Identify three Gothic elements in this chapter that establish the mood of the novel.

---

### Chapter 2 — First Day of School

**Focus**
Scout's first day of school is a disaster. Miss Caroline Fisher, the new teacher from Winston County, doesn't understand Maycomb's ways. She scolds Scout for already knowing how to read, and the Cunningham family's pride becomes a lesson in poverty and dignity.

**Key Details**
- Miss Caroline: young, inexperienced, from Northern Alabama — doesn't fit in
- Scout can read and write — Atticus taught her by reading together each night
- Miss Caroline: "Your father does not know how to teach"
- Walter Cunningham refuses lunch money he can't repay — Cunningham family pride
- Miss Caroline tries to force Walter to accept a quarter; Scout explains
- Scout gets punished for "telling on" the teacher's ignorance
- The irony: Scout is smarter than the teacher but gets in trouble for it
- Theme: formal education vs. the education of home and experience
- Burris Ewell introduced (briefly): the Ewell children only come to school one day a year

**Study Task:** Contrast the education Scout receives at home (from Atticus) with what she experiences at school. What is Lee suggesting about different kinds of intelligence and teaching?

---

### Chapter 3 — Climb Into His Skin

**Focus**
Scout catches Walter Cunningham for lunch at the Finch house, Calpurnia teaches Scout a lesson in hospitality, and the Ewell family is introduced. Atticus gives Scout the most important advice in the novel: empathy.

**Key Details**
- Scout beats up Walter for getting her in trouble — Jem invites him to dinner
- Calpurnia's lesson: "Don't matter who they are, anybody sets foot in this house's yo' comp'ny"
- Walter pours syrup on his food — Scout is horrified, Calpurnia gets angry
- Atticus's compromise: keep reading with him, but keep going to school
- Burris Ewell: "cootie" infestation, comes to school once a year
- Bob Ewell: town drunk, doesn't work, children neglected — society tolerates it
- "You never really understand a person until you consider things from his point of view... until you climb into his skin and walk around in it"
- The Ewells vs. the Cunninghams: both poor, different kinds of people

**Study Task:** Atticus's "climb into his skin" advice is the novel's moral center. How does Scout apply (or fail to apply) this principle in this chapter? How does Atticus model it himself?

---

### Chapter 4 — The Knothole Gifts

**Focus**
Scout discovers gum and polished pennies in the Radley oak's knothole — someone is leaving gifts. The children invent the "Boo Radley" game, acting out the Radley family history.

**Key Details**
- First knothole gift: Wrigley's Double-Mint gum (Scout is suspicious at first)
- Second gift: two polished Indian-head pennies ("Indian-heads: emblem of the land")
- The knothole is in the Radley oak tree on the Radley property line
- The "Boo Radley" game: Jem, Scout, Dill act out Radley family stories
- Scout feels the game is wrong but can't articulate why
- Atticus catches them playing but doesn't stop it directly
- Dill's role: the primary instigator, the imagination behind the Radley obsession
- "I sometimes think of the Radley tree" — retrospective reflection, foreshadowing
- The gifts connect Boo to the children

**Study Task:** What do the knothole gifts suggest about Boo Radley's personality and intentions? Why does Scout feel uneasy about the Boo Radley game?

---

### Chapter 5 — Miss Maudie and the Note

**Focus**
Miss Maudie Atkinson provides a more humane perspective on Boo Radley. The children attempt to deliver a note to Boo through his window but are caught by Atticus.

**Key Details**
- Miss Maudie: neighbor, friend to children, shares Atticus's moral clarity
- "Mockingbirds don't do one thing but make music for us to enjoy" — first hint of the title
- Maudie knew Boo as a boy — "I remember Arthur Radley when he was a boy"
- Boo's father was a "foot-washing Baptist" — extremely religious, kept Boo inside
- Boo's teenage "crime": associating with the wrong crowd, a minor legal incident
- Children try to deliver a note via fishing pole — Atticus catches them
- Atticus: "Stop tormenting that man"

**Study Task:** Compare Miss Maudie's version of Boo Radley with the children's monster version. Why does Atticus stop them from delivering the note?

---

### Chapter 6 — Jem Loses His Pants

**Focus**
On Dill's last night in Maycomb, the children sneak to the Radley house to peek through a window. They're spotted, Jem loses his pants, and returns later to retrieve them — finding them mysteriously mended.

**Key Details**
- Dill's last night before returning to Meridian
- The children creep to the Radley house, look through a loose shutter
- A shadow of a man with a hat appears — they run
- Jem's pants catch on the fence — he abandons them to escape
- Nathan Radley fires a shotgun — neighbors gather
- Atticus asks: "Don't you think you might have waked the whole neighborhood?"
- Jem goes back at 2 AM to retrieve his pants
- Jem returns "moody and silent" — won't tell Scout what happened
- First major clue: Boo is doing something to connect with them

**Study Task:** Jem's pants were "sewed up" and "folded across the fence" when he retrieved them. What does this tell us about Boo? Why does Jem keep this secret?

---

### Chapter 7 — The Knothole Cemented

**Focus**
The knothole gifts continue — soap carvings of Scout and Jem, a pocket watch, twine. But Nathan Radley cements the knothole, cutting off Boo's connection to the children.

**Key Details**
- Jem finally reveals: his pants were mended and hung neatly on the fence
- More gifts: gray twine, soap carvings (two figures that look like Scout and Jem)
- More gum, a medal, an old pocket watch with an aluminum knife
- The soap carvings are unmistakably them — Boo has been watching and caring
- Jem writes a thank-you note → finds the knothole filled with cement
- Nathan Radley: "Tree's dying. You plug 'em with cement when they're sick"
- Atticus notes the tree looks healthy — Jem understands Nathan lied
- Jem's tears: his first real encounter with adult cruelty
- "He wanted to stay connected" — Jem understands Boo's loneliness

**Study Task:** Nathan Radley cements the knothole to prevent Boo from communicating with the children. What does this act reveal about Nathan? Why does Jem cry?

---

### Chapter 8 — Fire and the Blanket

**Focus**
It snows in Maycomb — a rare event — and Miss Maudie's house burns down. In the chaos, Boo Radley places a blanket on Scout's shoulders without her noticing.

**Key Details**
- First snow in Maycomb since 1885 — school canceled
- Jem and Scout build a snowman (mud base, snow cover)
- Miss Maudie's house catches fire overnight
- Scout watches from the Radley gate, wrapped in a blanket
- Atticus: "Boo Radley. You were so busy looking at the fire you didn't know it when he put the blanket around you"
- Scout is stunned: "I damn near died"
- Miss Maudie: not upset about losing her house — she'll have more yard for flowers
- "Always wanted a smaller house, Jem. Gives me more yard"
- Symbolism: fire destroys but also cleanses
- Maudie's optimism: courage in the face of loss
- The blanket = Boo's care, his presence they never feel

**Study Task:** Scout didn't feel Boo approach. What does this say about his character — his gentleness, his stealth, his care? Miss Maudie responds to the fire with optimism — what does this reveal about her?

---

## PART TWO: The Mockingbird Theme (Chapters 9–11)
*Atticus takes the Tom Robinson case. The novel's central themes — courage, prejudice, and the mockingbird — are explicitly introduced.*

### Chapter 9 — Atticus Takes the Case

**Focus**
Atticus agrees to defend Tom Robinson, a Black man accused of raping a white woman. Scout faces taunts at school and at the family Christmas gathering.

**Key Details**
- Scout fights Cecil Jacobs for saying Atticus "defends niggers"
- Atticus: "I'm simply defending a Negro"
- "I couldn't hold my head up in town" — Atticus explains his conscience
- "The main one is, if I didn't I couldn't hold up my head in town"
- Christmas at Finch's Landing: Uncle Jack, Aunt Alexandra, Francis
- Francis calls Atticus a "nigger-lover" — Scout punches him
- Uncle Jack punishes Scout without hearing her side
- Atticus: "When a child asks you something, answer him"
- Atticus's legal strategy: the Ewells are lying
- Link Deas: "Won't you have a seat, Mr. Finch?" — respect in the community

**Study Task:** Atticus says he couldn't "hold up his head" if he didn't defend Tom. Is he guided more by his conscience, his reputation, or his sense of duty? What's the difference?

---

### Chapter 10 — It's a Sin to Kill a Mockingbird

**Focus**
Jem and Scout think Atticus is old and useless — until he shoots a rabid dog with one shot, revealing he was once "One-Shot Finch." The title phrase is introduced.

**Key Details**
- Jem and Scout are embarrassed by Atticus — he's old, doesn't do anything
- Tim Johnson (rabid dog) staggers down the street
- Heck Tate gives the rifle to Atticus — won't shoot it himself
- Atticus: one shot, instant kill
- Miss Maudie: "Your father's the deadest shot in Maycomb County"
- Atticus used to be "One-Shot Finch"
- He stopped hunting because he had an unfair advantage over animals
- "It's a sin to kill a mockingbird" (Miss Maudie explains)
- Mockingbirds: they don't harm anyone, they just sing
- Jem: "Atticus is a gentleman, just like me!"

**Study Task:** Connect the title to this chapter. What makes a mockingbird innocent? Who are the potential "mockingbirds" in the novel so far — characters who harm no one and only do good?

---

### Chapter 11 — Mrs. Dubose and Real Courage

**Focus**
Mrs. Dubose insults Atticus for defending Tom. Jem destroys her camellia bushes and is sentenced to read to her daily. She dies, and Atticus reveals she was fighting morphine addiction — the bravest person he ever knew.

**Key Details**
- Mrs. Dubose: cantankerous, racist, old and ill
- Calls Atticus a "nigger-lover" in front of Jem and Scout
- Jem destroys her camellia bushes with Scout's baton
- Punishment: read to Mrs. Dubose every day for a month
- Mrs. Dubose's fits: "When she was in one of her fits, she'd drool"
- She is dying and addicted to morphine — wants to die free
- Atticus: "She was the bravest person I ever knew"
- "Real courage is when you know you're licked before you begin but you begin anyway and see it through no matter what"
- Mrs. Dubose dies — leaves Jem a camellia in a box
- The camellia: forgiveness and recognition of her real victory
- Jem learns: courage is not about physical strength

**Study Task:** Compare Mrs. Dubose's courage with Atticus's. What is Atticus's definition of "real courage"? How does this redefine what it means to be brave?

---

## PART THREE: The Trial (Chapters 12–21)
*From Calpurnia's church through the trial itself. The children witness the full machinery of Maycomb's racial justice system.*

### Chapter 12 — Calpurnia's Church

**Focus**
Dill doesn't come for the summer. Jem is growing moody. Calpurnia takes the children to First Purchase African M.E. Church, where they witness the Black community's faith and resilience.

**Key Details**
- Dill stays in Meridian — Scout misses him
- Jem is 12 — moody, tells Scout to "act like a girl"
- Calpurnia takes them to First Purchase African M.E. Church
- Lula objects: "You ain't got no business bringin' white chillun here"
- Reverend Sykes welcomes them: "We don't have split churches"
- Calpurnia code-switches: talks differently at church vs. at Finch house
- "It's not necessary to tell all you know" — Calpurnia's lesson on discretion
- Tom Robinson's family attends this church
- Helen Robinson can't find work because of the accusation
- The Black community is united, supportive, generous (collection for Helen)

**Study Task:** Calpurnia speaks differently at church than she does at the Finch house. Why? What does this reveal about navigating a segregated society?

---

### Chapter 13 — Aunt Alexandra Arrives

**Focus**
Aunt Alexandra moves in to provide "feminine influence" for Scout. She brings Finch family pride, Southern tradition, and tension with Atticus.

**Key Details**
- Alexandra arrives to help raise the children (especially Scout)
- Obsessed with "gentle breeding" — family heritage, social status
- The Finches are "ancient and rich" — actually just old, not wealthy
- Alexandra wants Scout to be a lady — wear dresses, be polite
- She wants Atticus to fire Calpurnia — Atticus refuses
- "Alexandra, Calpurnia's not leaving this house until she wants to"
- Atticus tries to enforce Alexandra's wishes — it doesn't work
- Jem: "I think I'm beginning to understand why Boo Radley stays shut up... it's because he wants to stay inside"
- Scout notices: Atticus is different when Alexandra is around

**Study Task:** Jem says he understands why Boo stays inside — "he wants to." What does Jem mean? How does Alexandra's arrival make the Finch house feel different?

---

### Chapter 14 — Dill Returns

**Focus**
Dill runs away from home and hides under Scout's bed. Tension between Atticus and Alexandra grows. Bob Ewell threatens Atticus.

**Key Details**
- Dill runs away from Meridian — hides under Scout's bed
- Dill's parents "don't want me" — he feels unwanted
- Jem breaks the childhood "code" by telling Atticus about Dill
- Atticus handles Dill's situation calmly, feeds him
- Alexandra vs. Atticus: "You'd better take Calpurnia's side"
- Atticus: "Calpurnia's not leaving this house until she wants to"
- Scout almost sleeps in Jem's room — he's protective, she's scared
- Bob Ewell threatens Atticus publicly
- Dill: "They do things... they just do things" (about his parents)

**Study Task:** Why does Jem break the "code of silence" and tell Atticus about Dill? How does this mark Jem's growing maturity?

---

### Chapter 15 — The Lynch Mob

**Focus**
A mob gathers to lynch Tom Robinson before the trial. Atticus waits at the jail to stop them. Scout's innocence disarms the mob.

**Key Details**
- Heck Tate and others warn Atticus about potential trouble
- Tom is moved to Maycomb jail
- Atticus sits outside the jail, reading, guarding Tom
- The Old Sarum bunch (Cunninghams) arrive to lynch Tom
- The children follow Atticus and find him at the jail
- Scout recognizes Mr. Cunningham — starts a conversation about entailment
- Mr. Cunningham is shamed: "I'll tell him you said hey, little lady"
- The mob disperses — Scout unknowingly saves Tom
- Atticus: "I was so proud of you... He had to, even if it kills me"
- The power of naming: Scout calls Mr. Cunningham by name, makes him human

**Study Task:** Scout's simple conversation disarms the mob. How does this scene demonstrate the power of Atticus's "climb into his skin" lesson? Why does Scout succeed where adults would fail?

---

### Chapter 16 — The Trial Begins

**Focus**
The town gathers for the trial. Scout, Jem, and Dill sneak into the courtroom and watch from the Black balcony.

**Key Details**
- "It was a gala occasion" — the trial as entertainment and spectacle
- People from all over the county flood Maycomb
- Courtroom is segregated: Black gallery upstairs
- Reverend Sykes saves seats for the children in the colored balcony
- Judge Taylor: old, sleepy-looking, misses nothing
- Atticus is calm, methodical, respectful
- Dolphus Raymond: white man who prefers Black company
- The town's divide: public racism vs. private respect
- The trial as a reflection of Maycomb's social hierarchy

**Study Task:** Why does the town treat the trial as a public spectacle? How does the segregated courtroom reflect Maycomb's social structure?

---

### Chapter 17 — Sheriff Tate and Bob Ewell

**Focus**
Heck Tate testifies about Mayella's injuries — bruises on the right side of her face. Bob Ewell takes the stand, and Atticus reveals he is left-handed.

**Key Details**
- Heck Tate: Mayella was beaten, bruises mostly on her right side
- Bob Ewell testifies: vulgar, illiterate, aggressive
- "I seen that black nigger yonder ruttin' on my Mayella"
- Atticus makes Bob sign his name — reveals he is left-handed
- The Ewell property: trash-filled yard, poverty
- The implication: a left-handed man (Bob) could have caused the right-sided injuries
- "Mr. Ewell, are you ambidextrous?"
- Ewell can't read or write properly
- Atticus's strategy: build reasonable doubt through physical evidence

**Study Task:** Why is it significant that Mayella's bruises were on the right side of her face? How does Atticus use small physical details to dismantle Bob Ewell's testimony?

---

### Chapter 18 — Mayella Ewell Testifies

**Focus**
Mayella takes the stand — lonely, scared, and lying. Atticus treats her with courtesy but exposes the cracks in her story.

**Key Details**
- Mayella is 19: lonely, responsible for many siblings, no friends
- She accuses Tom of beating and raping her
- "Don't mock me" — she thinks Atticus is making fun of her
- Atticus is kind but persistent
- She asked Tom inside to "fix a door that didn't need fixing"
- Mayella admits there was "nothing wrong with the door"
- She refuses to answer what happened with her siblings
- "What did your father see in the window?" — she avoids the question
- Tom's crippled left arm: can't use it, can't have caused the injuries
- Mayella is both liar and victim: she's afraid of her father
- Scout realizes: "She was as sad as what Atticus said"

**Study Task:** Mayella is both the accuser and a victim. How does Lee create sympathy for her while still maintaining Tom's innocence? What is she afraid of?

---

### Chapter 19 — Tom Robinson Testifies

**Focus**
Tom takes the stand and tells his side: Mayella grabbed him, kissed him, and Bob Ewell caught them. Tom ran because he was afraid.

**Key Details**
- Tom testifies clearly, respectfully
- Mayella asked him to do chores regularly — always paid, no trouble
- The evening: Mayella asked him to fix a door — then grabbed him
- "She reached up and kissed me right on the mouth"
- Bob Ewell appeared at the window — screamed at Mayella
- Tom didn't fight back: "I was scared, suh... if I was to get in a fight I'd lose"
- Tom's left arm: crippled from a cotton gin accident at age 12
- He ran because of fear: "I was bound to go to jail"
- Link Deas: "I've never had a speck of trouble out of him"
- Dill starts crying — the injustice overwhelms him
- Tom's honesty vs. the weight of the system against him

**Study Task:** Tom is physically incapable of committing the crime (crippled arm), and the evidence supports his story. Why is the jury still likely to convict? What does Dill's crying represent?

---

### Chapter 20 — Atticus's Closing Argument

**Focus**
Atticus delivers his closing argument: the case is simple, the evidence is clear, but the jury must choose justice over prejudice.

**Key Details**
- Atticus's closing argument: "This case is as simple as black and white"
- The evidence: only two witnesses testify against Tom — the Ewells
- "The defendant is not guilty, but somebody in this courtroom is"
- The real crime: Maycomb's prejudice and Mayella's betrayal
- "All men are created equal" — but some think they're "more equal"
- "In the name of God, do your duty"
- Courtroom is dead silent — the jury leaves
- Dolphus Raymond's secret: his bottle contains Coca-Cola, not whiskey
- He pretends to be a drunk so people can excuse his lifestyle
- Raymond: "Let him get a little older... he won't cry"
- Dill's empathy is still strong — Raymond warns he'll lose it
- "It ain't honest but it's mighty helpful to folks"

**Study Task:** Analyze Atticus's "equal before the law" argument. What does he mean that all men are equal "in the courtroom" but not in society? How does Dolphus Raymond's secret connect to Atticus's argument?

---

### Chapter 21 — The Verdict

**Focus**
The jury returns after hours of deliberation — longer than expected. Verdict: guilty. Tom is convicted despite all evidence. The Black community stands as Atticus passes.

**Key Details**
- The jury is out for hours — Jem is hopeful: "We're gonna win"
- Calpurnia finds them in the balcony: they haven't eaten all day
- The jury returns — Atticus is sweating despite being "ice in the courtroom"
- Verdict: guilty
- Jem is devastated: "It ain't right"
- The entire Black gallery stands as Atticus walks out
- Reverend Sykes: "Miss Jean Louise, stand up. Your father's passin'"
- Atticus walks out alone, defeated but dignified
- Jem's first real encounter with injustice
- The children have lost their innocence about the justice system

**Study Task:** The jury deliberated longer than expected — a sign that at least one person almost held out. Why is this significant? What does the Black community's gesture (standing) mean?

---

## PART FOUR: Aftermath and Resolution (Chapters 22–31)
*The trial's consequences — Bob Ewell's revenge, Tom's death, and Boo Radley's final act of protection.*

### Chapter 22 — The Aftermath

**Focus**
The town reacts to the verdict. Miss Maudie consoles Jem, and Bob Ewell threatens Atticus.

**Key Details**
- Jem cries — his faith in the world is broken
- Miss Maudie consoles him: "There are some men in this world who were born to do our unpleasant jobs for us. Your father is one of them"
- Atticus is "the only man who can keep a jury out so long on a case like that"
- Bob Ewell threatens Atticus — spits in his face at the post office
- Atticus wipes his face and walks away — quiet dignity
- Maudie bakes a cake for the children
- Alexandra is frustrated with the town's ingratitude
- The irony: Atticus is hated for doing his job well

**Study Task:** Miss Maudie says some men are "born to do our unpleasant jobs." What does this mean about Atticus's role in Maycomb? Why does Bob Ewell spit on Atticus?

---

### Chapter 23 — Atticus on the Jury

**Focus**
Atticus explains that one person on the jury — a Cunningham — held out for Tom's innocence. Bob Ewell continues his threats.

**Key Details**
- Atticus: "The jury's a funny thing... they took hours because one man fought for you"
- That man was a Cunningham (one of the lynchers from Chapter 15)
- "A Cunningham might be stubborn but he won't take charity"
- "There's nothing more sickening to me than a low-grade white man who'll take advantage of a Negro"
- Bob Ewell says "he'll get Atticus if it takes the rest of his life"
- Atticus isn't worried: "It's not worth wasting fear over"
- Alexandra: Walter Cunningham can't come over — "He is trash"
- Scout: "I think I'd rather be a Negro than be so high and mighty"
- Boo Radley as the ultimate outsider — different kind of prejudice

**Study Task:** The Cunningham juror almost held out for acquittal. What does this say about "good people" in a flawed system? What are their limitations?

---

### Chapter 24 — The Missionary Circle

**Focus**
The Missionary Society meets at Finch house — the ladies discuss saving African natives while ignoring local injustice. Atticus brings news: Tom Robinson is dead.

**Key Details**
- Ladies of the Missionary Circle: Mrs. Merriweather, Mrs. Farrow, Miss Stephanie
- They discuss the "Mruna" tribe in Africa — Christianizing the savages
- Irony: obsessed with foreign missions, blind to local racism
- Mrs. Merriweather complains about "darkies in this town"
- Atticus interrupts: Tom Robinson is dead
- Tom tried to escape — shot 17 times
- "He took it as a matter of course" — Atticus's grief
- Alexandra keeps hosting: "I'll send them their refreshments"
- Scout learns: "If your aunt could be a lady in these circumstances, I could too"
- Miss Maudie's silent anger vs. the performative piety of the other women

**Study Task:** Contrast the missionary ladies' concern for the "Mruna tribe" in Africa with their prejudice against Maycomb's Black community. What is Lee satirizing?

---

### Chapter 25 — Tom's Death and the Mockingbird

**Focus**
Mr. Underwood writes an editorial comparing Tom's death to the "senseless slaughter of songbirds." The mockingbird theme is fully articulated.

**Key Details**
- Mr. Underwood's editorial: compares Tom's death to killing a mockingbird
- "He was trying to protect himself" — Tom ran, was shot
- The town: "Typical of a nigger to cut and run"
- "It was the first time I ever heard Atticus say it was a sin to do something and now I understood why"
- "In the secret courts of men's hearts Atticus had no case"
- Tom's death: 17 bullets for a man with one arm
- The mockingbird: Tom is innocent, killed for no reason
- The injustice becomes a permanent scar on the children
- Jem and Scout process the death differently

**Study Task:** Why is Tom a "mockingbird"? How does Mr. Underwood's editorial connect to the title of the novel?

---

### Chapter 26 — The Halloween Pageant

**Focus**
School resumes, and Scout prepares for the Halloween pageant. She notices hypocrisy: Miss Gates hates Hitler but is racist at home.

**Key Details**
- Scout is in third grade now
- School Halloween pageant: Scout is a ham (her costume is a ham)
- Miss Gates teaches about democracy vs. Hitler's persecution of Jews
- "We don't believe in persecuting anyone" — Miss Gates
- Scout overheard Miss Gates at the courthouse: "It's time somebody taught them a lesson"
- Scout: "Jem, how can she hate Hitler so bad and then turn around and be ugly to folks right at home?"
- Jem is furious: "Don't you ever mention that again!"
- Jem's reaction: he is traumatized, can't handle more injustice

**Study Task:** Miss Gates teaches democracy and anti-Nazism while being racist herself. What is Lee saying about hypocrisy? Why does Jem react so angrily to Scout's question?

---

### Chapter 27 — Bob Ewell's Revenge

**Focus**
Bob Ewell continues harassing anyone connected to the trial. The Halloween pageant preparations continue — tension builds.

**Key Details**
- Bob Ewell loses his WPA job — blames Atticus
- Judge Taylor's house: someone prowls outside (Ewell)
- Helen Robinson is harassed — Link Deas escorts her to work
- "He's a mean one... he'll do something"
- The Halloween pageant is a major event
- Scout is a ham — she feels ridiculous in her costume
- Jem walks Scout to the pageant
- Cecil Jacobs says he'll scare them on the way back
- Scout misses her cue because she fell asleep
- Unfinished pageant: Scout leaves in her ham costume

**Study Task:** Trace Bob Ewell's actions since the trial. What purpose does his character serve as a foil to Atticus? Why does the tension keep building?

---

### Chapter 28 — The Attack

**Focus**
Walking home from the pageant, Scout and Jem are attacked. Jem's arm is broken. Bob Ewell is dead.

**Key Details**
- Cecil Jacobs tries to scare them — anticlimactic
- The walk home: dark, quiet, leaves rustling
- Footsteps follow — they think it's Cecil
- Someone attacks: Jem is grabbed, arm broken, dragged
- Scout in the ham costume is protected (wire mesh saves her)
- A stranger pulls the attacker off Jem and kills him
- Bob Ewell is dead — stabbed with his own knife
- Heck Tate arrives, carries Jem home
- Scout sees a man carrying Jem: "Someone was staggering around"
- Scout doesn't realize she's safe
- Jem is unconscious but alive

**Study Task:** How does Lee create suspense in this chapter? What role does Scout's ham costume play in protecting her?

---

### Chapter 29 — The Reveal: Boo Radley

**Focus**
At Finch house, Scout finally meets Boo Radley face to face — the man who saved them is not a monster but a shy, gentle man.

**Key Details**
- Doctor tends to Jem — broken arm, unconscious
- Sheriff Tate: Bob Ewell is dead
- Scout realizes the quiet man in the corner is Boo Radley
- Boo's appearance: pale, thin, shy, young-looking despite being in his 30s
- "His lips were thin, but they had no other function"
- Boo asks: "Will you take me home?"
- Scout recognizes Boo from the soap carvings — he's real
- The contrast: monster vs. gentle man
- Boo hasn't spoken in public in years
- Scout is no longer afraid — she understands

**Study Task:** Describe the contrast between the "monster" Boo the children imagined and the real Boo. What is the effect of finally meeting him face to face?

### Chapter 30 — Standing in His Shoes

**Focus**
Heck Tate insists Bob Ewell fell on his own knife — protecting Boo from the public eye. Scout walks Boo home and stands on his porch, seeing the world from his perspective.

**Key Details**
- Heck Tate: Bob Ewell "fell on his knife" — case closed
- Atticus thinks Jem killed Ewell — wants to protect Jem
- Tate: "I never heard tell it was against the law for a man to try and save his own life"
- Tate insists Ewell's death stay quiet — to protect Boo
- "Let the dead bury the dead"
- Exposing Boo would be "like shootin' a mockingbird"
- Atticus understands: "I'd rather you shot tin cans in the back yard"
- Scout walks Boo home — he disappears inside
- Scout on the Radley porch: she sees the street from Boo's view
- "Atticus was right. You never really know a man until you stand in his shoes"

**Study Task:** Why does Tate insist on protecting Boo? Connect this to the mockingbird theme — who is the mockingbird in this chapter?

---

### Chapter 31 — The Final Lesson

**Focus**
Scout reflects on the novel's lessons as she returns home. Atticus reads to her, and the story ends with Scout safe, Jem healing.

**Key Details**
- Scout walks Boo home, stands on his porch
- "I had never seen our neighborhood from this angle"
- She reflects: Boo has been watching them all these years
- "One time he said you never really know a man until you stand in his shoes and walk around in them"
- Scout goes home, Atticus reads The Grey Ghost to her
- Atticus: "Most people are nice, Scout, when you finally see them"
- Scout falls asleep, Jem will recover
- "He turned out the light and went into Jem's room. He would be there all night, and he would be there when Jem waked up in the morning."
- Jem's arm will make him "ambidextrous"
- Boo Radley: found and lost in the same night
- The novel ends with safety, healing, and understanding

**Study Task:** Scout stands on the Radley porch and sees the world from Boo's perspective. What is the final lesson she learns? How has she grown from Chapter 1 to Chapter 31?
